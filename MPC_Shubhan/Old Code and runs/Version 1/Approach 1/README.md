## 1. The whole thing in plain words

No jargon in this section. Everything here is expanded properly further down.

### The situation

You have **one big battery on a truck** — the Mobile Charging Station, or MCS. You have a
couple of **electric excavators** parked at construction sites. Excavators dig, which drains
their batteries, and they can't drive to a charger. So the truck has to drive to *them*.

Your job is to plan the truck's whole day: where it goes, when it plugs into the grid to fill
up, when it drives to a site, which excavator it charges and for how long — and at the same
time plan what each excavator is *doing* every fifteen minutes: digging, loading, repositioning,
or resting.

You want the required digging and loading finished, nobody's battery flat or overfull, everyone
back to their starting charge by morning, and the electricity bill as small as possible.

### Why this isn't just one calculation

Two reasons.

**First, electricity isn't one price.** It's cheap at night, expensive in the late afternoon.
On top of the per-unit price you're charged for your single biggest spike of the month, and
charged *again* for your biggest spike during peak hours. So *when* the truck charges matters
enormously, and a plan that ignores this is expensive even if it does all the work.

**Second, and this is the real point: you don't know exactly how much power digging takes.**
It depends on the soil, the operator, the machine. You have a good estimate — call it 4.8 kW —
but reality varies around it. So any plan you make is built on a guess that will turn out
slightly wrong, and those small errors accumulate across a day.

### How the controller handles that

It doesn't try to plan the day once and hope. It plans, acts a little, looks, and re-plans:

> **Every 15 minutes:** work out the best possible plan for the rest of the day → do only the
> *first* 15 minutes of it → measure what actually happened to the batteries → throw the rest
> of the plan away → plan again from the real state.

That's Model Predictive Control. The plan is constantly wrong, and it constantly doesn't matter,
because it's constantly being rebuilt from what actually happened. Over a 24-hour day at 15-minute
steps, that's 96 full plans built and discarded to produce one day of decisions.

Each of those plans is a **MILP** — a big optimisation problem with yes/no decisions in it
(is the truck at this site? is the excavator digging?) plus continuous ones (how many kW?).
A solver called HiGHS finds the cheapest arrangement that satisfies every rule.

### Where the power estimate comes from

Before any of this starts, a **one-time statistical fit** (step 0) reads real recorded excavator
task data and works out, for each activity, the average power draw and how much it varies. That
average is what the planner plans on. The variation is what the simulated reality wobbles by.

Worth being precise about: the fit happens **once, before the run**, and then stays frozen. The
controller does *not* re-learn the power model as the day goes on. The machinery for online
re-fitting exists in the code but is deliberately not called.

### The honest bit about "reality"

There's no actual truck. "Reality" is simulated. So the code keeps two worlds strictly apart:

- **The planner** always believes digging takes exactly the average, say 4.8 kW.
- **The plant** (simulated reality) decides what *actually* happened this interval.

The plant has two settings, and this is the switch you'll flip most often — see §3.

### Comparing strategies fairly

To know whether re-planning every 15 minutes is *worth* anything, you need something to compare
against. So every run also computes a baseline:

- **Approach 0** — plan the entire day once at 8am, then just carry it out. Never re-plan, no
  matter what happens.
- **Approach 1** — the real controller, re-planning every 15 minutes.

Both face the **same** sequence of random numbers, drawn from one shared pool before either
starts. So any cost difference between them is down to strategy, not luck. That's the number
that tells you what re-planning bought you.

### Two flavours of controller

**Shrinking Horizon** plans one day. At 8am it plans 24 hours ahead; by noon it plans 20 hours
ahead; near the end it plans just the last interval. The window *shrinks*. Simple, and every
plan can see all the way to the finish line.

**Receding Horizon** handles several days. Its window is a fixed width that slides forward, so
it can always see roughly a day ahead — including into tomorrow. Work is issued as a per-day
quota that rolls over if a day falls behind.

### And finally

The **comparison driver** runs Approach 0, Shrinking, and Receding on identical input from one
shared random pool, and draws all three on the same charts.

**`RUN_ALL.jl`** runs the whole lot — both controllers on both datasets, both sensitivity
sweeps, and the comparison — in one click, with a single line at the top to switch plant mode.

## 2. The problem

One **MCS** (a battery on a towed truck) serves a fleet of **CEVs** (electric excavators) fixed
at construction sites. The MCS charges from **grid connection nodes** and discharges to CEVs at
**construction nodes**; it is towed between them, which costs both time and battery.

Over the scheduling horizon the controller chooses, per 15-minute interval:

- MCS grid charging power, per grid node
- MCS discharge power to each specific CEV
- MCS routing — depart / in transit / parked, with travel time and travel energy
- each CEV's activity — digging, loading+swinging, travelling (repositioning), or idle
- which CEVs are plugged in and charging

subject to battery physics and limits, plug counts, work quotas, digging-before-loading
precedence, an operator rest rule, travel pacing, and end-of-horizon energy targets. The
objective is time-of-use energy cost + monetised carbon + non-coincident demand charge +
on-peak demand charge + missed-work penalty + towing labour.

The uncertainty is in the **per-activity power draw**: digging, loading+swinging, travelling and
idling each pull a different, only approximately known number of kW.

## 3. Sampled vs mean mode

**This is the switch you will flip most often, so it gets its own section.**

The planner is unaffected by it. The MILP always plans on the mean `μ`. What changes is what the
simulated plant does when the plan is carried out.

| | `:sampled` | `:mean` |
|---|---|---|
| Realized power | next unused draw from the shared pool | pinned to the planning mean `μ` |
| Activity split within an interval | may be randomised (`multi_activity`) | the single planned activity, full interval |
| Realized vs planned | drifts apart | **identical, exactly** |
| Consumes pool samples? | yes, advances the cursor | **no**, cursor untouched |
| Needs a seed to reproduce? | yes | no |

### Why `:mean` exists

When the plan and reality agree perfectly, the cost you measure at the end **is** the cost the
optimiser promised at the start — there's nothing else it could be. So `:mean` gives you a
trustworthy reference: *this schedule costs $X*, with no randomness muddying it. It is the clean
reproduction of the deterministic single-shot model.

### What each choice measures

Approach 1 is **always** `:sampled`. Only Approach 0's plant mode changes. That means the mode
you pick decides what the reported gap actually means:

```
  A0(:sampled) ──► A1    =  value of re-planning against drift     (like-for-like plant)
  A0(:mean)    ──► A1    =  drift AND re-planning together         (the net figure)
```

Run the batch **both ways** and difference the two Approach 0 numbers: that difference is the
**cost of plan drift alone**, with no re-planning involved. Because a `:mean` run consumes no
samples, it can never perturb a `:sampled` run sharing the same pool.

### Where to set it

- **All stages at once:** the `PLANT_MODE` line at the top of `RUN_ALL.jl` (§6).
- **A single controller run:** `run_scenario_1(; approach0_plant = :mean, ...)`.
- **A sweep, standalone:** the `A0_PLANT` constant at the top of that folder's
  `run_soe_sweep.jl` (it defers to `MASTER_A0_PLANT` when the master runner sets it).
- **The comparison:** `run_comparison(; approach0_plant = :mean, ...)`.

### The diagnostic use

Both `run_mpc` and `run_one_shot` accept `plant`, so a **fully deterministic closed loop** is
available for debugging. With `plant = :mean` on both sides and `shrinking = true`, Approach 1
should reproduce Approach 0 exactly. If it doesn't, the cause is a closed-loop seam or carry-in
bug, not randomness — and no amount of reasoning about the stochastic plant will explain it.
This is the first thing to run whenever the two approaches disagree by more than you expect.

## 4. Shrinking vs Receding

Both are MPC in the general sense — re-solve, apply one interval, repeat. They differ in **how
long the planning window is and whether it spans more than one day.**

### Shrinking Horizon (single day)

- Plans the **entire remaining day** every step: the window is `[now … next 08:00]`, so it
  **shrinks** as the day advances — 96 intervals at 08:00, 1 interval at 07:45 next morning.
- **One day.** Work is a single **lumpsum** requirement per site.
- The overnight MCS recharge is scheduled **inside the same MILP** — no separate phase.
- Every window reaches the day boundary, so the terminal targets always bind.
- Simplest and fastest; the natural baseline.
- A fixed-`H` lookahead exists behind `shrinking = false`, but it is **experimental**: the
  terminal rules are gated on the window reaching day-end, so under a fixed `H` they vanish from
  all but the last `H` windows with nothing replacing them. Don't use it for reported results
  without adding a terminal cost first.

### Receding Horizon (multi-day)

- Plans a **fixed-width sliding** window — the rest of today plus `lookahead_days` further
  day-blocks — so the plan always sees into tomorrow.
- **Many days.** Work is a genuine **per-day schedule** (`dig_by_day` / `load_by_day`, optionally
  from `work_by_day.csv`). Unfinished work **rolls over** into the next day's backlog, and the
  roll-over is handled by the loop, outside the MILP.
- Simulates the reported days **plus one dropped buffer day**, purely so the last reported day
  still has a full day of lookahead. The buffer day is fully simulated, then discarded from all
  outputs — which matters for pool sizing (§5).
- Terminal targets are pinned at the **next day-start boundary**, so each window closes the
  current day's energy cycle.
- Like Shrinking, the day-block is the full 24 h, so nights are ordinary MILP intervals and the
  overnight recharge is scheduled by the same optimisation.

### At a glance

| Aspect | Shrinking | Receding |
|---|---|---|
| Horizon | single day, window shrinks | multi-day, fixed-width window slides |
| Lookahead | none beyond today | `lookahead_days` further day-blocks |
| Work demand | one lumpsum per site | per-day schedule, rolls over via a backlog |
| Buffer day | none | one extra day simulated then dropped |
| Terminal targets | at day-end | at each next-day-start boundary |
| Overnight recharge | inside the MILP | inside the MILP |
| Entry point | `6_Shrinking_Horizon_main.jl` | `6_Receding_Horizon_main.jl` |

**Which to use?** Shrinking for a clean single-day study or a fast baseline. Receding when work
spans multiple days, when the schedule should anticipate tomorrow, or when day-to-day carry-over
of work and energy matters.

## 5. The comparison driver

`Comparison/Code/7_Comparison_main.jl` runs **three** approaches on **one** dataset from **one**
shared random pool, and overlays them on merged figures.

- **Approach 0** — one-shot plan, executed open-loop (by default the Shrinking codebase's
  solver; `approach0_source = :receding` picks the other).
- **Approach 1a** — Shrinking Horizon closed loop.
- **Approach 1b** — Receding Horizon closed loop, run with `n_days = 1` so its single reported
  day is directly comparable to Shrinking's.

Three design points are worth understanding, because they are not obvious:

**Nothing is copied.** The driver `include`s both codebases in place from their own folders, so
edits there are picked up on the next run and no stale duplicate exists under `Comparison/`.

**Both codebases are wrapped in their own module.** They define identically-named submodules
(`Common`, `DataLoader`, `MCSModel`, `MPCLoop`, `Output`), so each is included inside a
`RecedingApp` / `ShrinkingApp` wrapper to stop one silently overwriting the other.

**One shared pool, via a deliberate alias.** Julia dispatches on nominal types, so a pool built
by one app's `Common` would be *rejected* by the other app's `run_mpc` even though the structs
are field-for-field identical. The fix: `ShrinkingApp` does not include its own `1_Common.jl` and
instead aliases `RecedingApp.Common`. This is safe — the two files differ only by three
multi-day helper functions that Shrinking never calls. With the alias, all three runs draw from
the literal same pre-generated sample sequence.

**Pool sizing.** `next_power!` **errors** when a cursor runs past the end of the pre-drawn
samples; it does not wrap. Because the Receding run always simulates a buffer day on top of its
reported days, the pool is sized `nK_day · (n_days_receding + 1) + 5`. Sizing on one day alone
would leave the Receding run roughly half the margin it can need, and the failure would surface
mid-run after the solver had already spent a long time working. Unconsumed samples cost nothing.

## 6. `RUN_ALL.jl` — the one-click runner

Place at `Approach 1\RUN_ALL.jl` and run:

```julia
include(raw"C:\Users\shubh\Desktop\MPC\Approach 1\RUN_ALL.jl")
```

or from a shell:

```bash
julia "C:\Users\shubh\Desktop\MPC\Approach 1\RUN_ALL.jl"
```

It runs seven stages in this order:

| # | Stage | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shrinking — `:input` | real-CSV dataset, single day |
| 2 | Shrinking — `:synthetic` | built-in dataset, single day |
| 3 | Shrinking — sweep | 10 runs varying `SOE_CEV_ini`, writes `summary.html` |
| 4 | Receding — `:input` | real-CSV dataset, `N_DAYS_RECEDING` days |
| 5 | Receding — `:synthetic` | built-in dataset, `N_DAYS_RECEDING` days |
| 6 | Receding — sweep | 10 runs varying `SOE_CEV_ini`, writes `summary.html` |
| 7 | Comparison | 3-way, into `Comparison/Output/` |

### The three edit points, all at the top of the file

They're wrapped in boxed comment banners so they're easy to find.

**1 — Plant mode.** The single line that switches everything:

```julia
const PLANT_MODE = :sampled          # <<<<<<  CHANGE THIS LINE: :sampled  or  :mean
```

This propagates to all seven stages — both controllers, both sweeps, and the comparison.

**2 — Which stages to run.** Comment out any line in `STAGES` to skip it. The two sweeps are by
far the slowest (10 full closed-loop runs each), so drop them for a quick pass.

**3 — Shared settings.** `SEED`, `TIME_LIMIT_SEC`, `MCMC_SAMPLES`, `H_SHRINKING`,
`SHRINKING_MODE`, `N_DAYS_RECEDING`, `RUN_REGRESSION`, `APPROACH0_SOURCE`, and the paths.

Set `RUN_REGRESSION = false` to skip the step-0 statistical fit and reuse whatever
`parameters.csv` already holds — much faster, and necessary if the task `.xlsx` folder isn't
present. The synthetic stages never run step 0 regardless.

### A note on `:synthetic` and the `synthetic_data` folders

`DataLoader.load_data(:synthetic)` returns `build_default_data()` — the scenario **hardcoded in
`2_DataLoader.jl`**. It reads no files. The CSVs under `data\synthetic_data\` are a
human-readable **mirror** of those same hardcoded values, kept so you can inspect the scenario
without opening the source. They are not an input, and nothing in either codebase reads that
folder; only `:input` takes an `input_dir`.

Two consequences worth remembering. If you change the synthetic scenario, edit
`build_default_data()` and update the mirror CSVs to match — a mirror that has silently drifted is
worse than none. And Receding's `synthetic_data\work_by_day.csv` mirrors the hardcoded
`dig_by_day` / `load_by_day` vectors, in which the leading entry is the grid node and carries no
work quota.

### Why each stage gets its own Julia process

This is deliberate and not optional. Both codebases define modules called `Common`,
`DataLoader`, `MCSModel`, `MPCLoop` and `Output`. Loading them into one session would have the
second silently redefine the first's modules, making results depend on load order. Separate
processes give each stage a clean namespace — and mean a stage that crashes can't take the rest
of the batch down with it.

### Output

Console output is streamed live **and** teed to `batch_logs/<stage>.log`. At the end you get a
summary table of per-stage status and wall time. A failed stage is recorded and the batch
continues.

## 7. File-by-file map

Both controller folders share the same layout; files are numbered in dependency order.

| File | Module | Role |
|---|---|---|
| `0_Regression.jl` | `Regression` | **Step 0.** Reads task `.xlsx` files, builds cumulative-ΔSoC energy-balance equations, fits `N(μ, σ)` per activity with NUTS, writes `p_*` and `sigma_*` into `parameters.csv`. Fail-soft if the folder is missing. `:input` only. |
| `1_Common.jl` | `Common` | Time/clock helpers, plot helpers, the Bayesian estimator type, and the `ActivityPowerPool` — the shared plant randomness. |
| `2_DataLoader.jl` | `DataLoader` | Loads the whole scenario into one immutable `NamedTuple d`. `:synthetic` (built-in) or `:input` (7 CSVs). |
| `3_MCSModel.jl` | `MCSModel` | `build_window_model(...)` — the window MILP, built and solved with JuMP + HiGHS. |
| `4_MPCLoop.jl` | `MPCLoop` | `run_mpc` (Approach 1, closed loop) and `run_one_shot` (Approach 0). Both share `apply_and_simulate!` for the apply/draw/advance step, so plant physics are identical and only the strategy differs. Both take the `plant` switch. |
| `5_Output.jl` | `Output` | Every artefact: figures, KPI and cost CSVs, solver diagnostics, replan grids, and the Approach 0 vs 1 comparison report. |
| `6_<name>_main.jl` | — | Thin orchestrator: `run_scenario_1(; mode, …)`. Tees the console to `run_log.txt`. Auto-runs unless `SCENARIO1_NO_AUTORUN` is set. |
| `run_soe_sweep.jl` | — | Sensitivity harness. Sweeps `SOE_CEV_ini` across 10 points at a fixed seed, keeps a subset of artefacts per run, writes `summary.html`. |

Plus, in `Comparison/Code/`: `7_Comparison_main.jl` (the driver) and `8_ComparisonOutput.jl`
(the merged 3-way figures and reports).

### The shared power pool

`draw_activity_power_pool` pre-draws `n_samples` values per `(entity, activity)` pair **before
any approach runs**. Each approach gets its own **cursor** into that same pool, so Approach 0 and
Approach 1 see identical numbers for identical occurrence sequences without one consuming the
other's. A draw is consumed only when that pair actually occurs in an interval — idle has `σ = 0`
and never consumes a slot. This is what makes the approach comparison a like-for-like measurement
rather than a comparison of two different random days.

## 8. The model, briefly

Equation numbers follow the source paper; full detail is in each subfolder's `math_model.tex`
and `constraints_code_vs_model.txt`.

- **(1) Objective** — energy + carbon + NC demand + OP demand + missed-work penalty + towing labour.
- **(2)–(4) Power flow** — aggregation, charge only at grid nodes, discharge only at sites, plug
  and acceptance caps, connection binaries.
- **(5) Work coupling** — work power capped by availability and forced to zero while charging;
  work power equals the chosen activity's constant draw; charge only while idle.
- **(6)–(7) Energy** — travel energy, MCS and CEV state-of-energy recursions with efficiency.
- **(8) Terminal & bounds** — MCS exact energy-neutral equality, CEV **floor** at its start level
  (a deliberate deviation: a CEV cannot discharge, so an exact equality can become unrecoverable
  once the stochastic plant lets one drift high), SOE ranges at every boundary.
- **(9)–(11) Routing** — presence partition, arrivals/departures, flow conservation, travel-time
  delay, park at a grid node by the day boundary.
- **(12) Work scheduling** — one activity per CEV per interval; the work quota; digging-before-
  loading precedence; the operator rest rule.
- **(13) Travel pacing** — a two-sided band tying repositioning to productive work, in applied
  interval counts (not hours), no tolerance.

**On the work quota.** It is written as a **balance equality**: work done + shortfall slack =
requirement outstanding. The shortfall is soft and priced, but because the slack is non-negative
the equality *also* implies a **hard upper cap** — a CEV cannot do more work than the requirement
still outstanding. "No working ahead" therefore comes out implicitly rather than as a separate
rule. Earlier revisions of the docs described this as an inequality with no cap; that was wrong,
and the source paper writes the equality too.

**MPC adaptations** beyond a single-shot solve, in both controllers: closed-loop carry-in of
measured state; rolling rules (rest, precedence, pacing) **seeded from the applied history** so a
work-run can't leak across re-solves; carried-in demand peaks; a generalised flow balance and
carried-in start position for a window that begins mid-route.

## 9. What each run writes

Into `<controller>/output/<synthetic|input>/`:

- numbered figures (PNG) and their backing CSVs — grid power, work profiles, MCS and CEV
  state-of-energy, prices and emissions, MCS location trajectory, per-MCS power
- KPI and cost reports
- per-window solver diagnostics and the worker schedule
- **replan grids** — what was planned at each re-solve versus what was ultimately done
- `approach0_vs_approach1.html` — the two approaches' fully realized outcomes side by side,
  **self-labelled by Approach 0's plant mode**, plus a run-diagnostics table (plant mode,
  infeasible windows, CEV SOE clamp count, solve time)
- `run_log.txt` — the full console output

Sweeps write into `<controller>/output/input_testing/`, one subfolder per sweep point plus
`summary.html`. The comparison writes into `Comparison/Output/`, including
`approach0_vs_shrinking_vs_receding.html` and merged 3-way figures with one colour per approach.

## 10. Known limits and open items

Stated plainly so nothing here is a surprise. Each subfolder's
`constraints_code_vs_model.txt` carries the full detail with code lines.

- **Only the CEV side of the plant is stochastic.** The MCS state is advanced by the *planned*
  value, so grid draw, travel loss and therefore every cost term follow the plan exactly. The
  disturbance enters through CEV depletion and reaches the objective only indirectly, via what
  the next re-solve decides to do about it. "The stochastic plant" should not be read as
  "everything is stochastic".
- **The CEV energy balance is clamped** to its SOE limits. The clamp is a numerical guard, not
  physics: when it binds, energy is silently created or discarded and the reported SOE stops
  being the integral of the reported power. This is invisible to the solver's feasibility status,
  so a run can report zero infeasible windows and still be physically inconsistent. Every event
  is now **counted and reported** (`n_clamped`) — a non-zero count qualifies that run's SOE
  numbers. Whether the right fix is to let the CEV go infeasible instead is still open.
- **No infeasibility fallback.** Every constraint except the missed-work slack is hard. An
  infeasible window makes the plant **hold state** for that interval, counted as `n_infeasible`;
  nothing is relaxed and no second solve is attempted.
- **The power model is not re-fit online.** Step 0 fits it once before the run and the loop holds
  it fixed. The online re-fit path exists but is deliberately not called.
- **Certainty-equivalent, not stochastic.** The MILP plans on the mean `μ`. The tracked per-
  activity `σ` is the hook for a future scenario-based stochastic MPC that would optimise over
  sampled powers instead.
- **Two accepted deviations from the source paper**, both MPC adaptations rather than errors: the
  flow-balance identity is generalised for a window that begins mid-route, and the start position
  is the carried-in node rather than a mandated grid node.
- **Minor, benign:** the missed-work slack is declared over all four activities but only digging
  and loading appear in a balance — the other two are dead variables driven to zero (the source
  paper has the same quirk); and self-loop travel binaries are never defined yet appear in the
  labour term, so presolve fixes them to zero.

## 12. Appendix — planning vs reality, explained at five levels

The single most important thing to understand about this codebase is the relationship between
**what the optimiser assumes** and **what the simulated world actually does**. Everything else —
the two approaches, the two plant modes, the whole comparison methodology — falls out of it.

This appendix answers one question at five increasing levels of depth. Read as far down as you
need and stop.

**The question:** *do we optimise using the mean and then subtract sampled power from the
batteries, or do we optimise using the sampled power too?*

**The answer, in one line:** we **always optimise on the mean**. What gets subtracted from the
batteries is a **sample**, except in `:mean` mode where that is also the mean.

---

### The master table

| | What the MILP optimises on | How often it re-solves | What the plant subtracts from CEV batteries | Realised = planned? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Approach 0, `plant = :mean`** | mean `μ` | **once**, at 08:00 (per day, in Receding) | **mean `μ`** | **Yes, exactly** |
| **Approach 0, `plant = :sampled`** | mean `μ` | **once**, at 08:00 (per day, in Receding) | **a sample** from the shared pool | No — drifts, and nothing corrects it |
| **Approach 1, Shrinking** | mean `μ` | every 15 min; window `[now → next 08:00]`, **shrinking** | **a sample** from the shared pool | No — but every re-solve starts from the true measured state |
| **Approach 1, Receding** | mean `μ` | every 15 min; **fixed-width** window sliding into tomorrow | **a sample** from the shared pool | No — but every re-solve starts from the true measured state |

Notice what does **not** vary down that first column. The optimiser never sees a sample, in any
row. The MILP is byte-identical across all four; only the plant and the re-solve cadence change.

---

### Level 1 — for a ten-year-old

You're packing lunch for a school trip. You *think* everyone eats one sandwich, so you pack one
each. That's the **plan**.

Then the trip happens. Some kids eat one and a half. Some eat half. Reality doesn't match your
guess — it never does.

Two ways to handle it:

- **Approach 0:** pack at 8am and don't look in the bag again all day. Whatever happens, happens.
- **Approach 1:** every fifteen minutes, peek in the bag, count what's actually left, and re-do
  the plan from there.

**Mean mode** is the pretend world where every kid eats *exactly* one sandwich, just like you
guessed. Nothing surprising happens. It's useful because it tells you how good your plan was,
without luck getting in the way.

---

### Level 2 — for a curious adult

The controller has one number for how much power digging takes: about 4.8 kW. It builds the whole
schedule around that number. It has no other information — it does not know what will actually
happen, and it is not allowed to know.

Reality wobbles. The simulator draws a slightly different number each time — 5.1, then 4.6, then
4.9. Batteries drain a bit faster or slower than the schedule expected, so by afternoon the real
world has drifted away from the paper plan.

Approach 0 built one schedule at 8am and follows it regardless. Approach 1 rebuilds the schedule
every fifteen minutes from the *real* battery levels, so drift gets caught and corrected while
it's still small.

Mean mode switches the wobble off. Reality obediently uses 4.8 kW every time, so the day plays
out precisely as planned. That gives you a clean reference cost with no luck in it.

**Why not just optimise on the sampled numbers?** Because those are the future. Feeding them to
the optimiser would let it plan around wobbles that haven't happened yet — which no real
controller could do. The number it would report would be a fantasy.

---

### Level 3 — for an engineer

Two strictly separated worlds, and the separation is the point.

**Controller side.** `build_window_model` receives `pvec = est.mu`, a fixed 4-vector of
per-activity powers. Work power is pinned by Eq. 5e, `P_work = Σₐ pₐ·u`, so the MILP's battery
trajectory is entirely determined by `μ`. The controller has no access to `σ` and none to the
pool. Structurally, it *cannot* see the disturbance.

**Plant side.** `apply_and_simulate!` applies interval `k₀` of the solved plan, then advances the
true state:

```julia
pt[a]      = use_mean ? pool.mu[a] : next_power!(pool, cursor, e, a)
work_true  = dot(a_real[e], p_true[e])
soe_cev[e] = clamp(soe_cev[e] + charged - work_true, SOE_min, SOE_max)
```

That ternary is the entire mode switch. Everything else — which activity ran, how the MCS moved,
how the objective is accounted — is shared code.

**The feedback loop.** Approach 1 passes the measured `soe_cev`, `mcs_node`, `rem_dig`,
`rem_load` and `hist` back into the next `build_window_model` call. Approach 0 never does; it
solves once and replays. That is the *only* structural difference between them.

**Fair comparison.** Both approaches draw from one `ActivityPowerPool`, pre-generated before
either runs, each with its own cursor. Identical occurrence sequences therefore see identical
numbers. The cost gap measures strategy, not luck — which is exactly what you want to report.

**Why `:mean` matters.** With realised equal to planned, the measured end-of-day cost *is* the
cost the optimiser promised at 08:00. There is nothing else it could be. So `A0(:mean)` gives a
trustworthy reference, and comparing it against `A0(:sampled)` isolates the cost of plan drift
with no re-planning involved.

---

### Level 4 — for a control engineer

This is **certainty-equivalent MPC**. The stochastic program

<pre>
min E[ J(x, u, w) ]   s.t.  x⁺ = f(x, u, w),  w ~ N(μ, σ)
</pre>

is replaced by its deterministic surrogate at `w = E[w] = μ`. The certainty-equivalence principle
is exact only for LQG; here the dynamics carry integer decisions and the constraints are hard, so
the surrogate is a heuristic — a good one, and the standard industrial choice, but its optimality
gap against the true stochastic optimum is not characterised.

Robustness comes from **feedback, not from the model**. The MILP is nominal; what handles the
disturbance is re-solving from the measured state every 15 minutes and applying only `u₀`. This
is textbook receding-horizon control and it is why the nominal model suffices.

The two horizon variants differ in terminal handling. **Shrinking** always reaches the day
boundary, so the terminal constraints (`SOE_MCS = SOE_ini` exactly, `SOE_CEV ≥ SOE_ini`) bind in
every window — recursive feasibility is inherited from the single-shot problem. **Receding** pins
the same targets at `b_term`, the next day-start, so each window closes the current day's energy
cycle. Neither carries a terminal cost or a value function; the constraints do all the work.
Which is precisely why `shrinking = false` is experimental: it drops the terminal rules from all
but the last `H` windows and replaces them with nothing, making the controller myopic.

**On the mode as an experimental instrument.** The decomposition

```
  A0(:mean) ──► A0(:sampled)   =  cost of open-loop plan drift
  A0(:sampled) ──► A1          =  value of feedback (re-planning)
```

separates two effects that a single A1-vs-A0 figure conflates. Run `run_mpc(d, pool;
plant = :mean)` and you get the fourth cell of the 2×2: a deterministic closed loop. With
`shrinking = true` it *must* reproduce `A0(:mean)` to the MIP gap, because both solve the same
problem from the same state on the same data. Any residual gap is a defect in the closed-loop
machinery — the history seeds, the carried-in position, the peak carry-in — and not a plant
effect. That is the diagnostic to reach for first whenever the approaches disagree.

---

### Level 5 — the honest caveats

Four qualifications that matter for anything you publish.

**Only the CEV side is stochastic.** `soe_mcs` is advanced by the *planned* value
`value(SOE_MCS[m, k₀+1])`, not by a simulated measurement. Grid draw, travel loss and hence every
cost term follow the plan exactly. The disturbance enters solely through CEV depletion and reaches
the objective only indirectly, through what the next re-solve decides about it. So the reported
cost variance understates what a fully stochastic plant would produce.

**The clamp breaks energy conservation.** When a sampled over-draw would push a CEV below
`SOE_min`, the guard silently restores it — energy from nowhere. The reported SOE then stops being
the integral of the reported power, and the MILP's feasibility status cannot see this. A run can
report zero infeasible windows and still be physically inconsistent. Events are counted as
`n_clamped`; **a non-zero count qualifies that run's SOE numbers.**

**Re-planning is not monotonically beneficial, and this codebase demonstrates it.** Each re-solve
is an independent MILP. A small state perturbation can flip a discrete routing binary, so the
applied trajectory is a concatenation of first-steps from 96 different optimisation problems
rather than a coherent plan. Nothing penalises churn between successive solutions. On the
synthetic dataset this shows up as Approach 1 accumulating substantially more MCS transit than
Approach 0 — the closed loop paying real money to chase disturbances that a committed plan simply
absorbed. Whether that is genuine or a seam defect is exactly what the `:mean` diagnostic above
settles, and it is **not yet settled**.

**A shared pool is a variance-reduction device, not a proof.** Common random numbers make a
single-seed comparison far tighter than independent sampling would, but one seed is still one
sample path. A cost gap smaller than the across-seed spread means nothing. For any claim about
the value of re-planning, sweep the seed and report a distribution — the machinery is already
there in `run_soe_sweep.jl`.
