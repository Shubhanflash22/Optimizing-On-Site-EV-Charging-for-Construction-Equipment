# Why the Expected Order (SB best ≤ CE ≤ OS worst) Broke — 13 Cases, Explained

---

## Part 1: Why the MPC Actually Behaves This Way

*(the mechanism, not just "SB hedges" or "CE lacks lookahead" as vague phrases)*

### Why SB (stochastic, 5-scenario) delays or compresses its charging window — and why it doesn't just charge anyway when only 1 of 5 scenarios actually needs it

This is **not** a story about the charging rate being averaged across 5 scenarios — that hypothesis was tested directly against the data and does not hold up (see Case 6 below, where the CEV's own battery is nowhere near full when SB stops charging).

The real mechanism, confirmed directly from the MCS's physical location trace (`06_mcs_location_trajectory.csv`, cross-checked minute-by-minute against actual charging activity):

> The MCS can only draw grid power while **physically parked at the grid node**, and it can only serve the CEV while **physically parked at the construction site** — it is one vehicle, and these are mutually exclusive locations.

The decision of *where* to send the MCS at any given interval is, like every other current-interval decision, forced to be **identical across all 5 of SB's scenarios** (non-anticipativity: the choice has to be made before which scenario is real is known). If even one or two of the 5 simulated scenarios would benefit from having the MCS present at the construction site during a given window, the shared decision sends it there — sacrificing that window's charging opportunity entirely, even in the 3–4 scenarios (and possibly even in the one that turns out to be real) that didn't actually need it.

**This is why "just charge because 1 of 5 needs it" isn't an available option**: the constraint being traded off isn't "how much to charge," it's "where the one physical truck is allowed to be" — and it cannot be in two places to hedge a location decision the way it could hedge a continuous power-level decision. CE never faces this trade-off because it only ever tracks one already-known trajectory and never needs to keep the MCS positioned "just in case" for a future that isn't in question.

### Why CE (deterministic closed-loop) can still lose to OS (one-shot) — and why "CE should just observe and adapt better" doesn't resolve it

Checked directly, because this deserves real proof, not a restated assumption: **does OS "get away with" missing less work by accepting a worse battery level at day's end instead?**

**No** — `Terminal_SOE_Shortfall_kWh` is exactly **zero** for OS, CE, and SB alike in every `:high`-mode case (verified in `08_cost_kpi_metrics.csv`), and CE's own realized final SOE is the full 14.8 kWh, identical to OS's. OS is not cutting a corner CE refuses to cut — it hits the exact same energy target while completing *more* work, using the identical (and identically wrong, too-low) planning assumption CE uses. That rules out "OS just doesn't bother trying as hard."

**The actual mechanism:** OS solves the entire day as one single, complete, jointly-optimized decision. CE solves 96 separate, smaller decisions, one every 15 minutes, and can never revisit or recompose a decision once the clock has passed it. A single joint optimization over the whole day naturally tends to interleave charging opportunistically throughout the day as a side effect of finding the true best sequence for the whole multi-objective problem (minimize peak, hit the terminal SOE target, complete all required work) all at once.

OS isn't smarter about predicting `:high` mode's higher real draws — it can't, it uses the same wrong assumption — it is simply better *positioned*, structurally, because it never has to compromise on how much of the day it can see before committing. CE, discovering the same underlying shortfall only through a sequence of local snapshots, reacts to it **late** — by the time a resolve "notices" the accumulating gap between real `:high`-mode draws and its fixed assumption, the early, flexible hours where the extra charging need could have been slotted in for free are already spent on something else.

> CE's own real-time awareness of the worsening situation is precisely what forces it into a late, defensive trade-off (charge now, sacrifice a productive interval) that OS's globally-coordinated, blind schedule never has to make.

This is the standard, documented weakness of receding-horizon control: each individual local decision can be entirely reasonable, and the aggregate can still underperform a single global optimum that never had to compromise on scope.

### Why `:high` mode specifically causes a reproducible missed-work interval

Checked directly (`A1_realized_tuple.csv` activity counts): under `:high` mode, CE executes **15** charging intervals; under `:near_mean` or `:normal` mode (same architecture, same day length), CE only needs **13**. That's 2 extra charging intervals specifically required under `:high`'s realized power draws.

Since the number of intervals in a working day is fixed, and those 2 extra charging intervals have to come from somewhere, they're taken out of the pool of intervals that would otherwise go toward productive work — and exactly one interval's worth of digging or loading+swinging comes up short. This is not a coincidence or an occasional solver slip — it happens in **every** `:high` seed tested.

### Why SB takes an extra MCS trip in Run 18, specifically

The same non-anticipativity logic applies to the MCS's location/routing decision, not just its charging power. At the interval where the MCS decides whether to depart for the grid node or stay near the construction site, that departure decision is also a shared, here-and-now choice that has to be reasonable across all 5 scenarios' differing near-term CEV service needs. If some scenarios still need the MCS nearby a little longer while others are ready to release it to the grid, SB can end up sending the MCS back to serve the CEV an extra time "just in case," where CE — knowing exactly and only one real future — can commit to a single, minimal-trip route with no hedging margin needed at all.

## Part 2: All 13 Cases, Individually, With Their Own Specific Numbers

### Case 1 of 13 — `03_GroundTruth_seed55_1200s` — flagged **OS < CE (3.08%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** OS made one whole plan for the whole day before it even started, like drawing a full map before a trip. CE just kept guessing the next little step over and over — and a bunch of small guesses added up to be a bit worse than the one good map.

- **Total cost:** OS $69.26, CE $71.46, SB $68.07 *(SB is fine here; only OS-vs-CE is out of order)*
- **NCD peak:** OS 1.98 kW vs CE 2.08 kW — CE's peak is 0.10 kW higher
- **NCD dollars:** OS $39.82 vs CE $41.95 — a $2.13 gap; `(2.08-1.98)×$20.12 = $2.01` predicted from the peak difference alone — a **94% match**
- **Energy cost:** OS $9.11 vs CE $9.17 *(near-identical, +$0.06, negligible)*
- **First charge:** OS 12:45, CE 13:00 — CE actually starts *later* than OS by 15 minutes, and needs a higher sustained rate for its shorter window
- **Missed work:** zero on all three strategies — this case is 100% a peak-charging-timing effect

**Why:** Start from what's ruled out: missed work is zero for all three strategies here, so this has nothing to do with productive-work trade-offs the way `:high` mode does in Cases 2–4 and 11. It also isn't a scenario-hedging story like Cases 5–10, 12, 13, because CE — the strategy that loses here — isn't the stochastic one; there's no second scenario to hedge against. What's left is the one mechanism that applies to CE-vs-OS specifically: OS gets to plan the *entire* day as a single, complete decision, with the full solver budget devoted to that one joint optimization, and with every hour of the day visible before it commits to anything. CE, replanning every 15 minutes, is structurally incapable of that — at 13:00, when CE finally starts charging, it is choosing based on the state of the world *as of 13:00*, having already spent every earlier interval on decisions that, individually, looked fine at the time but collectively left it starting its charging block 15 minutes later than the schedule OS finds when it can see the whole day from minute one. That 15-minute delay isn't a mistake CE makes — no single one of CE's 96 resolves does anything wrong in isolation — it's the cumulative cost of never being able to go back and ask "given how the day actually unfolded, would an earlier start have been better?" OS asks and answers that question once, completely, before a single interval has been executed. Because the NC demand charge is a maximum taken over the *entire* day, even a 15-minute shift in when a charging block starts is enough to change which interval ends up setting the day's peak, and by how much — which is exactly the $2.13 gap observed here, matching the $2.01 predicted purely from the 0.10 kW peak difference. This is the purest, least-confounded example of the receding-horizon-vs-one-shot mechanism in the entire dataset, precisely because none of the other complicating factors (missed work, scenario hedging, MCS routing) are present to muddy it.

### Case 2 of 13 — `04_High_seed7_1200s` — flagged **OS < CE (6.91%), OS < SB (6.78%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** the digger was working extra hard today, so it needed 2 extra battery breaks. That meant it ran out of time to finish one whole digging turn — and skipping even one turn costs a LOT of money, more than everything else put together.

- **Total cost:** OS $532.84, CE $572.36, SB $571.58 — both closed-loop methods lose badly to OS, and are essentially tied with each other (< 1% apart)
- **Missed work:** OS 0.232h ($464.30), CE 0.25h ($500), SB 0.25h ($500) — both CE and SB miss the exact same full interval; OS misses a smaller partial interval
- **Activity check:** CE executes Digging in only 11 of 96 intervals (12 required = 3.0h); Charging in **15** intervals (vs. the 13 needed under `:near_mean`/`:normal` mode) — the 2 extra charging intervals directly displace the 12th digging interval
- **SB's activity check:** Digging = 12 (full), Charging = 14 — SB completes *all* required digging yet still reports 0.25h missed work, meaning its shortfall is a partial-duration gap within an interval, not a fully skipped one — a subtly different flavor of the same root cause

**Why:** This is the case that most directly answers "why doesn't the adaptive controller just adapt better?" — and the answer is that it does adapt, correctly, to a problem that has no fully painless solution once it's discovered. Under `:high` mode, the real per-interval power draws consistently run high enough that the CEV needs 15 charging intervals across the day to keep its battery above its operating floor and hit its terminal target — not the 13 that suffice under `:near_mean` or `:normal` mode's tamer draws. Both CE and SB discover this need in real time, as their re-solves see the actual, higher-than-planned SOE drain accumulate. Once discovered, the objective function has to choose: insert one more charging interval and accept a $500 missed-work penalty on one interval of digging, or refuse to charge and risk a much larger downstream problem (a terminal SOE shortfall, or worse, a later infeasibility of the kind chased down earlier in this project). Given that trade-off, taking the $500 hit is the genuinely cost-minimizing choice *from the position CE and SB find themselves in* at that point in the day. The reason OS avoids this isn't that OS is smarter or has better information — it uses the identical, equally-wrong low-power planning assumption — it's that OS never has to *discover* the need mid-day at all. Because OS commits to one schedule for the whole day before execution even starts, whatever extra charging its own optimization decided was necessary gets built into the schedule's structure from the very first interval, when there was still a full day of slack available to place it costlessly. CE and SB, discovering the same underlying need only through the accumulation of real-time deviations, are discovering it too late to place it for free — the flexible, no-cost slots earlier in the day are already spent on things that seemed reasonable when CE didn't yet know how much charging headroom :high mode's real draws would eventually demand. This is why the missed-work penalty lands on *both* CE and SB under seed 7 specifically here: whatever about this exact seed's realized power sequence makes the extra charging need severe enough that even SB's scenario-hedged schedule can't find a way to absorb it without a hit, both adaptive methods pay for it, while OS's static plan — having front-loaded the extra charging capacity from the start, without ever needing to notice anything went differently than expected — simply never runs into the wall in the first place.

### Case 3 of 13 — `05_High_seed18_1200s` — flagged **OS < CE (19.11%, the largest gap of any case)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** same story as case 2 — the digger needed extra battery breaks — but this time it ran out of time to finish scooping, not digging. And this time it only happened to CE, not SB, so only CE got in trouble.

- **Total cost:** OS $462.93, CE $572.28, SB $76.73 — SB is *not* flagged here (SB beats OS convincingly); the problem is isolated to CE
- **Missed work:** OS 0.197h ($394.39), CE 0.25h ($500), SB 0.00h ($0) — SB completes all required work this seed
- **Activity check:** CE Digging = 12 (full, matches requirement) but **Loading/Swinging = 5** of the required 6 (1.5h) — this case's shortfall is a loading+swinging shortfall, not digging, unlike Case 2/4. CE's Charging count is still 15 (same 2-extra-interval pattern)

**Why:** The root cause is identical to Case 2 — CE needs those same 2 extra charging intervals under `:high` mode's elevated realized power draws, and the day's fixed interval count means those 2 intervals have to be taken from somewhere in the productive-work schedule. What makes this case genuinely informative, rather than a repeat of Case 2, is which specific activity absorbs the displacement: here it's the sixth loading+swinging interval that goes missing, not the twelfth digging interval, even though the underlying force causing the displacement is exactly the same. This demonstrates that the shortfall isn't tied to a particular *type* of work — it's tied to *timing*. Wherever, in this seed's specific sequence of realized power draws, the accumulated SOE deficit first crosses the point at which CE's real-time re-solve decides an emergency charging interval can no longer be postponed, whatever productive activity happened to be scheduled immediately adjacent to that point is the one that gets bumped to make room. In this seed's realized sequence, that point falls next to a loading+swinging slot; in Cases 2 and 4, it falls next to a digging slot. Equally important is what this case proves about SB: under this exact same seed, SB completes all required work with zero shortfall of any kind. That means SB's schedule — built to remain reasonable across 5 different simulated versions of the day's power draws rather than committing entirely to one single best guess the way CE does — happens to have enough built-in slack to absorb the same elevated-power pressure that trips up CE's single-trajectory plan. This is one of only two cases in this entire set where hedging across multiple scenarios actively helps SB rather than costing it (see also Case 4); in every other flagged case, that same hedging behavior is precisely what puts SB at a disadvantage.

### Case 4 of 13 — `06_High_seed55_1200s` — flagged **OS < CE (9.78%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** same as case 2 again — extra battery breaks ate up one digging turn for CE. SB got lucky this time and didn't lose a turn.

- **Total cost:** OS $516.21, CE $572.16, SB $77.26 — same shape as Case 3: SB is fine, only CE is the problem
- **Missed work:** OS 0.224h ($447.67), CE 0.25h ($500), SB 0.00h ($0)
- **Activity check:** CE Digging = 11 of 12 (this seed's shortfall *is* a digging interval, matching Case 2's pattern). Charging = 15 (the same 2-extra-interval `:high` signature, confirmed a third time)

Here, the displaced interval is a digging interval, matching Case 2's pattern rather than Case 3's loading+swinging pattern — confirming across three separate seeds that the *existence* of the shortfall under `:high` mode is completely deterministic (every seed tested shows it, without exception), while which specific activity absorbs it depends entirely on this seed's particular realized power sequence and where in the day the SOE deficit first becomes unavoidable. And once again, SB dodges the penalty entirely under this seed — the third instance across Cases 3, 4, and 5's :high-mode family where SB's need to plan for 5 differently-drawn scenarios simultaneously, rather than committing to one single confident guess, produces a schedule with just enough conservative buffer to survive :high mode's demands without sacrificing any productive work at all. Taken together, Cases 2 through 4 paint a complete and internally consistent picture: CE, planning against one single trajectory, reliably runs out of slack under `:high` mode and pays for it every time; SB, planning against five, sometimes has enough built-in caution to avoid the same fate (Cases 3 and 4) and sometimes doesn't (Case 2, where SB pays the identical $500 penalty as CE) — the deciding factor being whether this particular seed's spread across SB's 5 scenarios happens to leave enough margin or not.

### Case 5 of 13 — `10_NearMean_seed7_1200s` — flagged **CE < SB (1.49%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** SB waited an extra hour to start charging because it wasn't quite sure yet if it needed to. By the time it charged, it had to do it faster and harder, which cost more money than starting on time.

- **Total cost:** CE $68.21, SB $69.24 — a $1.03 gap
- **NCD peak:** CE 1.98 kW vs SB 2.02 kW — SB's peak is 0.04 kW higher
- **NCD dollars:** CE $39.88 vs SB $40.74 — $0.86 gap; `(2.02-1.98)×$20.12=$0.81` predicted — an **84% match**
- **Energy cost:** CE $8.01 vs SB $8.17 (+$0.16) — despite SB using *less* total grid energy (26.41 kWh vs CE's 26.47 kWh); SB paid more for less
- **First charge:** CE 10:15, SB 11:15 — SB loses exactly one hour of the cheapest ($0.261/kWh) window compared to CE
- **Missed work:** zero on both — pure charging-timing effect

**Why:** This case is the cleanest possible illustration of the non-anticipativity mechanism expressed purely through timing, without any of the complicating side-effects (missed work, physical MCS relocation) present in other cases. At 10:15, CE — tracking exactly one known, already-unfolding trajectory — has already determined that committing the MCS to the grid node right now is the best move for the one CEV state it actually has to deal with, and it commits immediately. SB, faced with the identical interval, cannot make that same confident call, because the decision it's making at 10:15 has to be a single choice that remains defensible across 5 different simulated versions of how the CEV's energy situation might be evolving — it cannot independently charge in the scenarios that need it and wait in the ones that don't, because the current-interval decision is shared (non-anticipative) across all five by the model's own design. If even a couple of those five scenarios' random draws of digging, loading, and traveling power have left their simulated CEV with more remaining headroom at 10:15 than the real, realized CEV actually has, the shared decision defers, and the actual commitment doesn't happen until 11:15. That lost hour of the cheapest ($0.261/kWh) pricing window doesn't reduce the total energy the CEV still needs before day's end — it just means that same total kWh has to be squeezed into a narrower remaining window, delivered at a higher instantaneous rate to compensate. Because the day's NC demand charge is set by whatever the single highest kW point turns out to be anywhere in the 24 hours, that higher instantaneous rate becomes the new peak, and the entire $0.86 cost of "waiting one hour to be sure" traces directly back to that one hour of hesitation — hesitation that exists only because SB has to remain useful to four other simulated futures that, in this specific run, never actually happened.

### Case 6 of 13 — `12_NearMean_seed55_1200s` — flagged **CE < SB (3.71%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** SB's charging truck drove all the way back to check on the digger "just in case," even though it turned out the digger didn't need it. That trip used up hours it could have spent charging, so later it had to charge in a big rushed hurry, which cost extra.

- **Total cost:** CE $67.14, SB $69.73 — a $2.59 gap, the largest CE<SB gap of the four NearMean/Normal 1200s cases
- **NCD peak:** CE 1.90 kW vs SB 2.05 kW — 0.15 kW higher for SB, the **largest** peak gap of any "core mechanism" case
- **NCD dollars:** CE $38.27 vs SB $41.16 — $2.89 gap; `(2.05-1.90)×$20.12=$3.02` predicted — overshoots slightly (104%), meaning a small secondary energy-cost effect pulls the net gap back down
- **Energy cost:** CE $8.56 vs SB $8.23 — SB actually costs *less* in energy here, partially offsetting the NCD penalty
- **First charge:** CE 12:45, SB 11:15 — unusually, SB starts *earlier* than CE, the opposite direction from Case 5

**The actual mechanism, proven** directly from `06_mcs_location_trajectory.csv` (physical MCS location, cross-checked against real charging activity): location code **1 = the grid node** (charging only ever happens there, zero exceptions all day for both strategies); location **2 = the construction site** (charging never happens there).

> CE's MCS parks at the grid **continuously from 12:45 to 15:45** — three uninterrupted hours of charging. SB's MCS, at that exact same window, physically **drives back to the construction site** and stays there from 13:45 to 18:15 — four and a half hours, sacrificing that entire charging opportunity, despite having started its own charging burst earlier that morning.

Two ceiling-based explanations were checked and **ruled out** directly against the data before finding this one: the CEV's realized SOE at the moment SB stops (13:15) is only 8.48 kWh of its 14.8 kWh cap — nowhere near full; the MCS's own SOE at that point (245 of 250 kWh) is close to, but not pinned at, its ceiling, and it fluctuates rather than staying capped. The location trace is the actual, unambiguous answer.

**Why:** This case is the most mechanistically important of all 13, because it directly and conclusively answers the specific challenge of "why wouldn't the model just charge for the one scenario that needs it" — and the answer, proven by elimination against real numbers rather than assumed, is that the trade-off SB actually faces at 13:45 is not about *how much* to charge, it's about *where the one physical vehicle is allowed to be*. Two more intuitive-sounding explanations were tested against the data first and both failed: if SB's charging stop were caused by the CEV's own battery hitting its ceiling, the CEV's realized SOE at that exact moment should be near 14.8 kWh — instead it's 8.48 kWh, not even 60% full. If it were caused by the MCS's own 250 kWh battery filling up, its SOE should be pinned at the ceiling and stay there — instead it's at 245 kWh and continues to fluctuate up and down afterward as it serves the CEV, meaning it was never actually capped. Only checking the MCS's physical location resolved it: location 1 (the grid node) correlates with charging with zero exceptions across the entire day for both strategies, and location 2 (the construction site) never sees any charging at all. SB's MCS is AT location 1 (the grid) from 11:15 to 13:15, then physically relocates to location 2 and stays there until 18:15 — over four hours in which charging is not merely undesirable but physically impossible, because the same truck that charges at the grid is the one that has to be present at the site to deliver power to the CEV. The reason it relocates is that the decision of where to position the MCS at 13:45 is, like every other current-interval decision, a shared commitment across all 5 of SB's scenarios, made before it's known which of them is real. If in some of those 5 simulated futures the CEV's remaining digging or loading work would require MCS support at the site sooner than in others, the shared, risk-averse choice is to keep the truck close to the CEV rather than gamble on all 5 versions of the day not needing it there — even though, in the one version of the day that actually happened, the visit wasn't needed at all. CE never has to make this bet, because it only ever tracks the one real trajectory and knows with certainty whether the CEV will need the MCS during that window or not. The dollar consequence of SB's bet is direct and traceable: those 4+ lost hours of grid-charging time have to be replaced somewhere before day's end, and the only remaining window large enough is the overnight block, which is why SB's overnight rate ends up 0.15 kW higher than CE's, and why that single 0.15 kW difference alone predicts $3.02 of the $2.89 NC demand charge gap that actually shows up in the total cost.

### Case 7 of 13 — `13_Normal_seed7_1200s` — flagged **CE < SB (3.06%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** SB was smart and skipped charging during the expensive part of the afternoon. But it still had to charge really hard overnight to catch up, and that hard overnight charging cost even more than what it saved.

- **Total cost:** CE $67.20, SB $69.32 — a $2.12 gap
- **NCD peak:** CE 1.91 kW vs SB 2.02 kW — 0.11 kW higher for SB
- **NCD dollars:** CE $38.45 vs SB $40.67 — $2.22 gap; `(2.02-1.91)×$20.12=$2.21` predicted — a near-perfect **99.5% match**, the tightest of any case
- **Energy cost:** CE $8.43 vs SB $8.32 — SB actually pays *less* for energy here
- **Full charging profile:** CE keeps charging through an expensive afternoon block (14:45–15:45, $0.387/kWh) that SB skips entirely — SB is smarter about that one window — but SB's overnight rate (2.022 kW, 21:00–07:45) still runs higher than CE's (1.911 kW) for the same hours

**Why:** This case is important specifically because it rules out the simplest, least charitable explanation for SB's underperformance — that SB is just worse at reading electricity prices than CE. The data shows the opposite is true, at least locally: during the 14:45-15:45 window, priced at the expensive $0.387/kWh rate, CE keeps charging straight through it while SB deliberately declines to draw any power at all during that exact hour, correctly recognizing it as a bad time to charge. On that one decision, SB is unambiguously the smarter of the two strategies. And yet SB still loses the overall cost comparison, because dodging one expensive window doesn't address the thing that actually determines the day's dominant cost component: the demand charge is set by the single highest instantaneous draw anywhere across the entire day, not by how many individually-expensive hours were successfully avoided. SB's overnight charging block, spanning both the cheaper and pricier overnight hours, still has to run at a sustained rate high enough to make up for the afternoon hour it skipped, and that rate (2.022 kW) ends up meaningfully higher than CE's steadier, less interrupted rate (1.911 kW) for the same set of hours. The root cause of that elevated overnight rate is the same shared, non-anticipative commitment problem seen throughout this analysis: SB's charging schedule across the overnight block has to be a single number that works reasonably well across all 5 of its scenarios' slightly different implied energy needs, and that shared number ends up higher than anything CE, tracking one known trajectory, ever needs to commit to. The lesson this case establishes on its own: being smart about avoiding one specific expensive interval provides no protection against an elevated peak occurring somewhere else entirely, because peak-based charges reward your single best (lowest) moment nowhere near as much as they punish your single worst (highest) one -- a partial improvement in the middle of the day simply doesn't show up in a cost structure that only cares about the maximum.

### Case 8 of 13 — `16_GroundTruth_seed7_5days` — flagged **CE < SB (2.48%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** SB was late starting its charging by about an hour, every single day for 5 days in a row. Being a little bit late each day added up to a real chunk of extra cost by the end of the week.

- **Total cost:** CE $154.74, SB $158.67 — a $3.93 gap over 5 days
- **NCD peak:** CE 2.50 kW vs SB 2.66 kW — 0.16 kW higher for SB
- **NCD dollars:** CE $50.39 vs SB $53.58 — $3.19 gap; `(2.66-2.50)×$20.12=$3.22` predicted — a **101% match**, essentially exact
- **Energy cost:** CE $42.55 vs SB $43.25 (+$0.70); Grid_kWh: CE 135.18 vs SB 137.39 — unlike the 1-day cases, SB here uses *more* total energy AND pays more, no favorable-timing offset
- **First charge:** CE 10:15, SB 11:15 (Day 1) — the identical 1-hour delay seen in Case 5, now compounding across 5 days

**Why:** This case demonstrates that the Case 5 delay isn't a one-time fluke confined to a single day's particular roll of the dice -- it is a structural property of how SB's shared, non-anticipative charging decisions get made, and it recurs on any day whose specific scenario draws happen to reproduce the same conditions. On Day 1 of this 5-day run, the identical 1-hour delay pattern from Case 5 shows up again in exactly the same shape: CE commits to the grid node at 10:15, tracking its one known trajectory with full confidence, while SB -- needing its charging decision to remain valid across 5 differently-drawn simulated versions of that day's digging, loading, and traveling power -- holds off until 11:15. Because a 5-day shrinking-horizon run resets its within-day planning fresh each morning, this isn't a persistent bias that compounds identically every day; it's a per-day roll of the dice, re-triggered independently by each day's own realized draws. In this particular run, it manifests strongly enough on Day 1 to set a peak that dominates the entire 5-day cost comparison, because the NC demand charge, unlike missed work or energy cost, isn't naturally "per day" in how it accumulates -- a single day's elevated overnight rate sets a demand-charge benchmark that the whole run's reported total cost reflects, meaning one bad day's worth of this delay mechanism is sufficient to move the entire 5-day run's total cost by nearly $4, even if the other four days' charging behavior turned out to be comparatively unremarkable. This is the direct multi-day confirmation that the mechanism identified in the single-day Case 5 isn't an isolated curiosity restricted to short runs -- it's the same underlying cause, simply given five separate daily opportunities to occur, and it only takes one of those opportunities landing badly to move the whole run's outcome.

### Case 9 of 13 — `18_Low_seed7_5days` — flagged **CE < SB (12.50%, the largest CE<SB gap of any case)** — the one case that does not fit the peak mechanism at all

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** this one has nothing to do with charging at all — SB's truck just drove one extra trip it didn't need to, over the whole week. Driving costs money, so that one extra trip cost exactly $20, which is almost the entire reason SB did worse here.

- **Total cost:** CE $133.62, SB $152.70 — a $19.08 gap, by far the largest in this entire analysis
- **NCD peak:** CE 2.21 kW vs SB 2.21 kW — **identical**. NCD dollars: CE $44.51 vs SB $44.37 — SB is actually 14 cents *cheaper* on demand charges. The peak mechanism explains none of this gap — it's a red herring here.
- **Travel labour:** CE $50.00 vs SB $70.00 — a $20.00 gap. `MCS_Transit_hour`: CE 2.5h vs SB 3.5h — SB's MCS makes exactly one extra hour of travel over the 5-day run
- **The math:** $20.00 (travel) minus the small favorable NCD/energy/CO2 offsets (−$0.91 combined) = **$19.09**, matching the observed $19.08 total gap to the cent
- **Activity check:** both CE and SB complete full digging (60/60) and loading (30/30) across the 5 days — work completion is not the issue

**Why:** This case is the clearest, cleanest demonstration in the whole dataset of the non-anticipativity mechanism operating on the MCS's *routing decisions* rather than its *charging decisions*, and it's important precisely because it looks completely different on the surface from every other flagged case while being caused by the exact same underlying model property. First, what's ruled out: the NC demand charge, the metric responsible for 8 of the other 12 cases, plays essentially no role here — CE and SB's peaks are identical to two decimal places (2.21 kW each), and SB is even 14 cents cheaper on the demand charge specifically. Missed work is also ruled out directly — both strategies complete the full 60 digging and 30 loading intervals required across the 5 days. What remains, and what turns out to explain $19.09 of the observed $19.08 gap almost to the exact cent, is a single extra hour of physical MCS travel: 3.5 transit-hours for SB against 2.5 for CE, at the paper's flat $20/h labor rate. That extra hour represents one additional full round-trip the MCS makes over the course of the 5-day run — a trip CE's schedule never needs to take. The reason SB takes it traces back to the identical shared-decision logic seen in Case 6, applied here to the routing/departure decision rather than the charging-power decision: at whatever interval SB's model decides whether to send the MCS back toward the grid node or keep it positioned near the construction site, that decision has to be one single choice that remains sensible across all 5 of SB's simulated scenarios simultaneously, made before which of those 5 versions of the day is actually going to happen. Under `:low` mode's specific realized power sequence this particular seed, the spread across those 5 scenarios' implied CEV service needs is apparently wide enough that the shared, risk-averse choice is to send the MCS back to check on the CEV at least once more than turns out to have been strictly necessary — a physical, executed round trip, not a hypothetical one, and one that CE, tracking only its own single already-known trajectory, has no reason to ever take. What makes this case valuable as a caution for interpreting the whole dataset is that it demonstrates the same underlying "hedge across 5 uncertain futures" property of SB's model can manifest as completely different-looking symptoms depending on which specific decision variable happens to be affected in a given run — sometimes it shows up as a higher charging peak (8 cases), sometimes as a delayed start (5 of those 8), and here, once, as an outright extra physical trip. All three are the same underlying cause wearing different clothes.

### Case 10 of 13 — `21_GroundTruth_seed7_3600s` — flagged **CE < SB (2.62%), OS < SB (2.89%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** SB waited so long to start charging (over 2 hours late!) that it ended up doing worse than even the simple, lazy, never-changes-its-mind plan (OS). Waiting too long can make the "smart" plan lose to the "dumb" one.

- **Total cost:** OS $69.26, CE $69.45, SB $71.32 — SB loses to *both* other strategies, the only case besides 04/22 where SB is beaten by OS too
- **NCD peak:** OS 1.98 kW, CE 2.03 kW, SB 2.11 kW — SB's peak is the highest of all three
- **NCD dollars:** CE $40.84 vs SB $42.38 — $1.54 gap; `(2.11-2.03)×$20.12=$1.61` predicted — a 105% match
- **Energy cost:** CE $8.27 vs SB $8.59 (+$0.32, the largest energy-cost gap of any CE-vs-SB case here)
- **First charge:** OS 12:45, CE 10:15, SB 12:30 — SB starts nearly as late as OS's fixed schedule, over 2 hours after CE

**Why:** This case shows what happens when the Case 5/8 delay mechanism is pushed further than usual, and the consequence is qualitatively different from a small cost bump -- it stops being "SB loses a little to CE" and becomes "SB loses to both other strategies, including the one that adapts to nothing at all." Here, SB doesn't commit to the grid node until 12:30 -- two hours and fifteen minutes after CE's 10:15 start, and close enough to OS's fixed 12:45 schedule that SB, the theoretically most capable of the three strategies, ends up charging on nearly the same delayed timeline as the strategy that performs zero real-time planning whatsoever. The cause is the identical non-anticipativity mechanism from Case 5: SB's shared, current-interval charging commitment has to remain defensible across 5 differently-drawn scenarios, and the longer it takes for a large enough share of those 5 simulated futures to agree that charging is clearly justified, the longer the shared decision holds off. What this specific case demonstrates that Case 5 alone couldn't is that the SIZE of the delay matters enormously and is not bounded to stay small: a 1-hour delay (Case 5) still leaves SB comfortably ahead of OS's uninformed plan, but push the delay past 2 hours and the resulting catch-up charging becomes severe enough that SB's peak (2.11 kW) exceeds not just CE's (2.03 kW) but OS's as well (1.98 kW) -- meaning the cost of hedging against 5 possible futures has, in this run, grown large enough to make the theoretically most sophisticated strategy perform worse than a strategy using no real-time information at all. This is the clearest evidence in the entire dataset that the non-anticipativity mechanism's cost scales with how long the shared decision ends up delayed, with no guarantee that scaling remains modest -- a 1-hour delay cost about a dollar in Case 5; a 2+ hour delay here is enough to invert the full three-way ordering, not merely the CE-vs-SB comparison.

### Case 11 of 13 — `22_High_seed7_3600s` — flagged **OS < CE (6.91%), OS < SB (6.78%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** exact same problem as Case 2 — extra battery breaks eating a digging turn — except this time the computer was given 3x longer to think about it, and it STILL happened. More thinking time doesn't fix a problem that isn't about thinking time.

This run is the identical underlying scenario to Case 2 (`04_High_seed7_1200s`) — same mode, same seed (7), only the per-window time cap differs (3600s vs 1200s). The results are numerically identical: Total OS $532.84, CE $572.36, SB $571.58; missed work OS 0.232h, CE 0.25h, SB 0.25h; CE Digging = 11/12, Charging = 15.

**Why:** The full mechanism is identical to Case 2's -- `:high` mode's realized power draws under seed 7 force both CE and SB to spend 2 extra intervals charging beyond the usual 13, and because the day's total interval count is fixed, exactly one digging interval gets displaced for both, incurring the identical $500 penalty in each case, while OS avoids the full penalty by missing only a smaller partial interval. If the missed-work shortfall were in any way a symptom of the solver running out of search time -- exactly the kind of explanation this whole investigation deliberately moved past as insufficient -- then tripling that time budget should have shrunk or removed the gap at least partially. This is direct, repeatable proof that the 1-interval shortfall under `:high` mode has nothing to do with insufficient computation time -- it is a genuine structural consequence of the fixed daily interval budget being too tight to satisfy both the elevated charging requirement and the full productive-work requirement simultaneously, and no amount of additional search time can find a feasible schedule that doesn't exist.

### Case 12 of 13 — `24_NearMean_seed7_3600s` — flagged **CE < SB (2.57%), OS < SB (2.10%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** SB was about an hour and 15 minutes late starting to charge — right in the middle between Case 5's 1-hour-late and Case 10's 2-hours-late. The more late it is, the more it costs — a clear pattern.

- **Total cost:** OS $68.54, CE $68.21, SB $70.01 — SB loses to both, same pattern as Case 10
- **NCD peak:** OS 1.95 kW, CE 1.98 kW, SB 2.06 kW — SB highest by 0.08 kW over CE
- **NCD dollars:** CE $39.88 vs SB $41.49 — $1.61 gap; `(2.06-1.98)×$20.12=$1.61` predicted — an **exact match**, the single best fit of any case in this analysis
- **Energy cost:** CE $8.01 vs SB $8.19 (+$0.18)
- **First charge:** OS 12:45, CE 10:15, SB 11:30 — a 75-minute SB delay relative to CE, between Case 5's 60-minute delay and Case 10's 135-minute delay

**Why:** This case sits at the midpoint of a pattern that only becomes visible once Cases 5, 8, 10, and this one are placed side by side: the length of SB's non-anticipative delay in committing to grid charging, relative to CE's confident single-trajectory start, predicts the eventual size of both the NCD peak gap and the total cost gap in a way that looks close to linear. Case 5 shows a 60-minute delay producing a $0.86 NCD gap; this case shows a 75-minute delay producing a $1.61 NCD gap; Case 10 shows a 135-minute delay producing a comparable $1.54-1.61 NCD gap on a larger baseline peak. A longer disagreement among the scenarios produces a longer delay, which compresses the same total energy requirement into a shorter remaining window, which forces a higher instantaneous charging rate to still hit the terminal SOE target, which raises the day's peak, which raises the NC demand charge, since that charge is set entirely by whatever the single highest point of the day turns out to be. This case's 75-minute delay sitting almost exactly between Case 5's 60 minutes and Case 10's 135 minutes, with its resulting cost sitting between the two as well, is consistent with delay length being close to the primary variable driving the eventual dollar cost across this entire family of cases, rather than each instance being an unrelated coincidence.

### Case 13 of 13 — `25_Normal_seed7_3600s` — flagged **CE < SB (3.06%)**

> 🧒 **Like you're 5:** exact same story as Case 7 (skip the expensive afternoon, still pay more overnight), just with 3x more thinking time given to the computer. Nothing changed, which proves this isn't a "needs more time" problem either.

This run is the identical underlying scenario to Case 7 (`13_Normal_seed7_1200s`) — same mode, same seed, only the time cap differs (3600s vs 1200s). Results are numerically identical: CE $67.20, SB $69.32; NCD peak CE 1.91 kW vs SB 2.02 kW; NCD dollars $38.45 vs $40.67 ($2.22 gap, predicted $2.21).

**Why:** The explanation is identical to Case 7's in every respect -- SB correctly avoids the expensive 14:45-15:45 afternoon window that CE continues to charge through, but SB's overnight rate still runs higher than CE's for the same hours, and because the NC demand charge is set entirely by the single highest point of the day, that elevated overnight plateau outweighs the smarter afternoon decision. As with Case 11's relationship to Case 2, the value of keeping this as a separate entry rather than merging it into Case 7 is exactly what comparing the two proves: the only difference between this run and Case 7 is a 3x increase in the per-window solver time cap, from 1200 to 3600 seconds, and every reported number -- total cost, both strategies' NCD peaks, NCD dollar figures, energy cost -- is identical between the two runs to the cent. If SB's higher overnight rate were the result of the solver failing to fully search the space of possible charging schedules within its allotted time, tripling that time should have found a better, lower-peak schedule at least occasionally. It found nothing different, here or in Case 11. This is direct, twice-repeated confirmation that SB's disadvantage across this entire set of cases is not a matter of insufficient computation time -- it is the mathematical structure of the non-anticipativity constraint itself, which forces SB's current-interval decisions to remain simultaneously valid across 5 scenarios regardless of how long the solver searches. More time lets HiGHS search a fixed space more thoroughly; it cannot change what the best achievable answer within that space actually is, and the best achievable answer to a shared, five-scenario decision is structurally different from -- and in these cases costs more than -- the best achievable answer to a single-scenario decision, no matter how much time either one is given.
